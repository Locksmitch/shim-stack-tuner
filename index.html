Shim Stack Tuner

An independent, open, offline shim-stack & damping-force calculator for shock/fork valving — inspired by the classic Shim ReStackor workflow. Runs entirely in your browser, no install, no internet needed.

Quick-set all dimension fields: Every geometry & shim field has its own mm/in dropdown, so you can mix parts from different manufacturers — a metric damper body with imperial shims, for example. This selector is just a bulk shortcut; individual fields always stay overridable.
About this tool & its limitations (read before trusting the numbers)

This is an independent, from-scratch calculator. It is not affiliated with, and does not reproduce, the proprietary commercial "Shim ReStackor" software — that program's exact finite-element and fluid-dynamics algorithms are closed-source, licensed IP. This tool implements its own simplified, physically-grounded engineering model:

Stack stiffness: the shim stack is modeled as a tapered cantilevered disc — at each radius, the local bending stiffness is the sum of cubes of the individual engaged shim thicknesses, Σ(count·t³), because loose shims slide freely against each other and each bends about its own neutral axis. (A "welded" laminate would use the cube of the total thickness — enormously stiffer, and it ranks stacks wrongly: it makes many thin shims beat a few thick ones. Real inter-shim friction adds back some stiffness above this free-sliding lower bound, so absolute forces may read a little soft.) The deflection is integrated incrementally in small force steps so shims can lock in partway through the force range. Two things make a shim start disengaged: an explicit Float value (a deliberate air gap you enter, 0 by default), or a structural crossover the tool detects automatically — a wider shim sitting above a narrower one (say a wide clamp plate over a small pivot shim) is separated from the bending stack by a cavity equal to the missing shim's thickness, and only locks in once the stack beneath bends far enough to close that cavity. Either way the engagement is permanent for the rest of that curve (no unloading/hysteresis modeled — realistic for a monotonic quasi-static push, not for full load/unload cycles), and engagement is binary: a partially-contacted shim's gradual stiffening is approximated as a single switch. List shims in physical stacking order from the valve face outward — with structural detection, order now matters. Most trustworthy for comparing configurations rather than as an absolute finite-element result.

Damping force: flow through the valve port is modeled with the orifice equation, coupled iteratively with stack deflection. The discharge coefficient is reduced at low Reynolds number using a simple smooth correction (Cd_eff = Cd · Re/(Re+Re₀)) so thicker oil and slower shaft speeds correctly show more viscous, less inertial behavior — this correction is an engineering approximation, not a citation from a specific paper.

Oil viscosity vs. temperature: if you enter both cSt@40°C and cSt@100°C, the tool interpolates/extrapolates to your chosen oil temperature using the standard ASTM/Walther viscosity-temperature equation.

Target & optimizer: turn on "Target curve" to drag a desired force curve over the chart, then "Optimize to target" searches this valve's real shim catalog (the exact OEM parts, filtered to the valve's ID and OD bounds) for stacks whose curve best matches your target. It hill-climbs from your current stack — swapping thicknesses, ODs, counts, adding/removing shims, and reordering them — and returns the best few as "Opt" curves you can apply. Crucially it explores shim order, so it can discover crossover/pivot arrangements (a narrow pivot behind the face shim to soften low-speed damping while a wider backup shim further down holds high-speed) rather than only simple tapers — the same technique you'd build by hand to control curve shape. The face shim (widest, at the valve) covers the ports, so its OD is locked to the OEM's port-covering size for that valve and always kept as the piston-contacting shim; the optimizer tunes its thickness and count but never shrinks or drops it. Stack height is a soft target relative to the loaded stock tune (default ±0.05mm, may go slightly over but never under, per the RockShox-style rule), and each suggestion is flagged in-band / over / under. It searches coarsely then re-ranks the finalists at full resolution. It's only as good as the physics below (free-sliding stack, binary shim engagement, no inter-shim friction), so treat a suggestion as a strong dyno starting point, not a final answer.

Not modeled yet: clicker/bleed circuits, mid-valve + base valve combined/cavitation effects, suspension response. Treat absolute numbers as estimates; use it mainly to see how changes move the curve, and sanity-check against a dyno or real-world feel whenever possible.

Units: every length field (shim diameters/thicknesses, port geometry) has its own mm/in selector, so you can mix metric and imperial parts in the same build. Force/velocity/modulus and the output charts use a separate "Results & range units" selector in the Calculation range panel. Saved config files always store canonical values so they reload correctly regardless of what units you were viewing at save time.

Reference diagram — what to measure

Simplified damper cross-section — not to scale.

The shim ID is just the center hole (must clear the shaft). The clamp diameter — the OD of the clamp washer / piston land that pins the stack — is what the shims actually bend off: only the material outside this diameter flexes, so it's the true inner boundary of the bending model. Make the clamp diameter larger to shorten the bending span (stiffer), smaller for a longer, softer span. It must be smaller than the largest shim OD and at least the shim ID.

⠿▾Shim stack & damping force

CountDiameterThicknessFloatgapUnit

List shims from the valve face outward — group identical shims using the Count column. Each row has its own unit, so a metric shim and an imported inch shim can sit in the same stack. If your stack has a separate clamp washer and/or nut, add them as regular rows too.

Float is normally 0 (shim always bonded/engaged, the default). Give a "backup" or crossover shim a Float > 0 and it stays disengaged — contributing nothing — until the stack's local deflection right at that shim's own radius reaches the Float value, at which point it locks in and stiffens the stack from then on. That's what lets a stack be genuinely soft at low speed and stiffen at high speed (or vice versa), not just scaled up or down.

Shim #1 sits on the valve face; bigger/thicker shims near the face stiffen the stack most, since fluid pressure loads the outer edge near the port.
